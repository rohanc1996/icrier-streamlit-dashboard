# The Vintage Problem in Innovation Rankings

*Why the year behind an indicator matters as much as the number itself — and what index producers should do about it.*

Innovation rankings shape budget speeches, investment pitches and ministry talking points. But a composite index is a chain of choices: what to measure, how to normalize it, which year to use, and how much weight to give each indicator. We recently ran an indicator-level test on 18 indicators from the World Intellectual Property Organization's (WIPO) Global Innovation Index (GII) across 71 economies. The exercise was a quality-control check before we used the numbers ourselves. The most persistent problem was not an outlier or a scaling quirk. It was age.

A ranking implies a common point in time, but the underlying data rarely delivers one. Within a single indicator, some countries report recent values while others lag by years, and the index blends them as if they were contemporaneous. Market capitalization is the clearest case. Forty-six of our 71 economies have a 2025 value. But Italy and the Slovak Republic are recorded at 2014, the Netherlands at 2017, France, Ireland, Portugal, Belgium, Ukraine and Algeria at 2018, and Norway at 2019. That is an eleven-year spread inside one indicator.

Gross expenditure on R&D tells a similar story. Thirty-nine economies have 2023 data and twelve have 2024. Yet India, Indonesia, Colombia and the Philippines are still recorded at 2020; Nigeria at 2019; Ethiopia at 2017; and Ecuador at 2014. Patent applications are almost complete at 2024, but Nigeria's value is from 2020. IP receipts are mostly 2025, but the Dominican Republic's is from 2022.

This matters because the rank looks precise even though the comparison is not like-for-like. If a country's 2020 R&D intensity is set against another's 2024 value, with no adjustment for the four years in between, the index is not measuring a moment but a patchwork.

It is worth separating this from missing data, because the two problems need different responses. Market capitalization covers 62 of our 71 economies. Gross R&D expenditure covers 67, low-carbon share 62, and IP receipts 67. Missing observations announce themselves, while a value from 2014 does not. It enters the average looking exactly like a value from 2025: weighted the same, scaled the same, ranked the same. The reader sees a number, not its age. For an indicator, that hidden gap can matter more than the difference between two adjacent countries.

None of this is necessarily the fault of the people who build the index. I understand that the vintage of the underlying data is frequently outside the control of researchers. Statistical agencies publish on their own schedules; definitions change; some economies have thin reporting capacity; and WIPO itself depends on UNESCO, the World Bank and other collectors. Rebuilding a series from primary sources is not always possible, and dropping an indicator outright can cost more than keeping it. But even if the vintage cannot be fixed, the weighting can be adjusted to mitigate the impact.

That is where the current approach stops short. We checked WIPO's methodology and found that, for each economy, the GII uses only the most recent yearly data available, going back to 2014. Missing values are not estimated; they are marked "n/a" and left out of the aggregation, which does redistribute weight across the remaining indicators. This is fine, but a stale observation is not treated as missing. It stays in the mix at full weight, as if it were current.

WIPO's only leverage on weights is a scaling coefficient of 0.5 or 1.0, used to limit multicollinearity when indicators correlate above 0.95. There is no vintage adjustment. In practice, a 2014 value and a 2025 value carry the same weight in the same sub-pillar. In ICRIER's SIDE index, we take a different approach: when an indicator's data is stale, we reduce its weight. An old observation should inform the score, not dominate it. The same logic should apply here.

The GII remains useful. The issue is not that composite indices like it are worthless, but that they should be read as structured arguments rather than direct measurements. Three practical steps would help. It could begin by publishing the vintage of every country-indicator observation, not just the indicator's modal year or the final score. It could reduce the weight of stale observations, so an old value cannot count as much as a current one. And it could report sensitivity to indicator choice, vintage and weighting — ideally as a rank range rather than a single number.

A data audit is not an attack on an index. It is quality control. The first step for better innovation policy is better measurement of the status-quo. That requires one to ask not only what the measured numbers represent, but also how old they are.
