# Where the servers meet the water

When policymakers talk about the environmental cost of digital infrastructure, they usually mean electricity. That is understandable: a single new AI campus can draw as much power as a mid-sized city, and the grid connection is the constraint that makes the news. Water is the quieter constraint. Cooling towers evaporate it by the million gallons a day, and the basins that supply them are frequently the same basins already under pressure from agriculture and growing cities. The question worth asking is not how much water the digital economy uses in total, but whether the infrastructure is being built in places that can afford it.

That question is hard to answer at the level of national averages, which is why we built an indicator at the level of the watershed. It measures the share of a country's data-centre capacity that sits in water-stressed sub-basins, on a zero to one hundred scale where lower is better. It is a prototype for the 2027 SIDE edition and it is not yet part of the composite index.

The distinction between water stress and water availability matters more than it might appear. Water stress compares demand with the renewable supply actually available in a given place; availability compares a country's total resources with its population. Peru has roughly forty-nine thousand cubic metres of renewable freshwater per person, which places it among the most water-rich countries on earth, and on a national withdrawal-to-supply measure it scores about five per cent, better than almost everyone. Yet fourteen of its data centres sit in basins that our index places at the maximum, because Lima and its industrial belt occupy a coastal desert whose rivers are almost entirely committed. Chile shows the same pattern: forty-five thousand cubic metres per person, nine per cent nationally, and ninety-one out of a hundred on our measure. A country-level figure would have reported both as water-secure and missed the only fact that matters to an operator choosing a site.

For the water layer we used the World Resources Institute's Aqueduct 4.0, released in 2023. It divides the world into roughly sixty-eight thousand five hundred hydrological units formed by the intersection of sub-basins, provinces and aquifers, each carrying a withdrawal-to-supply ratio for a baseline period of 1979 to 2019, alongside groundwater decline, drought risk and projections to 2030, 2050 and 2080. Three properties made it the right choice. It is licensed CC BY 4.0, so it can be redistributed with attribution. It is fine enough to distinguish one side of a river basin from another. And every unit already carries an ISO country code, which removes an entire class of boundary-matching error. The alternatives were weaker for this purpose: the FAO's AQUASTAT reports at country level and cannot be intersected with a point, and Copernicus's drought observatory describes the timing of drought rather than the level of competition for supply.

For the data centres there is no equivalent. No free dataset records every facility on earth. We combined two. PeeringDB, the interconnection database maintained by network operators, gave us 5,912 facilities, of which 5,306 carry coordinates; it is current, global and free through a documented API, but it records peering presence rather than buildings, so it has no power or floor-area field and it systematically misses self-built hyperscale campuses that never join a public exchange. Epoch AI's frontier data-centre database gave us 96 large AI sites backed by measured power capacity, 14.3 gigawatts of it, derived from satellite imagery and permit filings under a CC BY licence. It is the only free source with real megawatts, but it covers only the largest sites. OpenStreetMap was the third option and proved too sparse outside Europe to carry the weight. The commercial alternatives, Baxtel and S&P's facility database, hold the capacity data we lack and are priced accordingly.

Joining the two layers is a point-in-polygon exercise, and it is where most of the engineering effort went. Each facility is placed in its Aqueduct unit and inherits that unit's stress score. Of the facilities with coordinates, 99.6 per cent landed inside a unit; the remainder sit offshore or on addresses that geocoded badly. We verified the join by re-testing a random sample of twenty-five facilities with an independent containment check that shares no code with the spatial join, which is how we caught a bug of our own earlier in the work: an early version requested geometry rounded to about a kilometre, which pushed facilities near a boundary into the neighbouring basin. At a site in Phoenix the rounded join reported a stress value of 0.30 against a true value of 1.89. Nothing in the aggregate output looked obviously wrong, which is the argument for sampling.

Scoring is deliberately simple. Aqueduct reports stress as a ratio and maps it to a zero-to-five score, so a basin using under ten per cent of its renewable supply scores near zero and one using more than eighty per cent scores near five. Each facility takes that score, the country takes the capacity-weighted average, and we rescale to a hundred. A one-gigawatt campus in a dry basin therefore counts for far more than a small colocation centre in a wet one. The index is not a total, and it does not reward or punish the size of a country's digital estate: three facilities in an extremely stressed basin produce the same score as three hundred. That is a choice, and it is the right one for a composite index, because an absolute measure would simply re-rank countries by how many data centres they have, which the connectivity and harness pillars already capture. We tested four weighting schemes, including counting every facility equally, and the ranking barely moved; the calibrated and unweighted scores correlate at 0.998.

The results are sharper than we expected. Sixty-five of the seventy-one SIDE countries can be scored; the median is 34 out of 100. The United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Qatar, Israel and Peru sit at the ceiling. Chile scores 91, Bangladesh 90, South Africa and Portugal 88, Spain 87. China is at 71, India at 67, Australia at 61, the United Kingdom at 47, Germany at 43 and the United States at 41. Singapore, Ghana, Rwanda and Hungary score zero. Algeria has no facility records at all, and five countries with fewer than three facilities are left blank rather than scored on too little evidence. Among the large AI campuses, 26 per cent of measured capacity, some 3.8 gigawatts, sits in basins using more than forty per cent of their renewable supply, and 9.4 per cent sits above eighty per cent.

The European results will surprise people. Vilnius sits in a basin at 0.70, Lisbon at 0.91, Athens at 1.15, all in Aqueduct's high or extremely high bands. That is not an error: much of Europe withdraws a large share of what its sub-basins actually hold, even where national resources look comfortable. Anyone expecting the map to show only the Gulf and South Asia should look at the Iberian peninsula first.

India is worth pausing on. Two hundred and three facilities are in the sample, the fifth-largest national count, and 64 per cent of them sit in basins above the forty per cent threshold. Chennai and Bengaluru are the extreme cases, at 15 of 15 and 12 of 13 facilities respectively, followed by Noida, Pune and Hyderabad. Mumbai, the largest single cluster with 33 facilities, is not in that group, because its coastal sub-basin is comparatively well supplied. A national figure cannot see any of this: it reports one ratio for a country where the answer differs by city, and sometimes by the side of a city.

The limits are worth stating plainly. The facility layer undercounts: China shows 27 facilities against a far larger real estate, and Algeria shows none. Power is measured for 93 sites; for the rest we used a fixed placeholder, which is defensible only because the ranking is insensitive to it. Fifty-one of the ninety-six sites are pinned to a city rather than a street address, and three could not be located at all. Thirty-six per cent of tracked capacity has no PeeringDB facility within thirty kilometres, so the two layers corroborate each other less often than we would like. Nor does this measure water consumption: cooling design drives that, and we observed a twenty-fold spread between sites of comparable size. It measures exposure, not litres.

What it implies is straightforward. Siting is a lever that countries control, through permitting, basin-level disclosure and cooling standards, in a way that they do not control rainfall. A country can be water-rich in aggregate and still be building its digital future in the wrong valley. This indicator is an attempt to make that visible, one basin at a time.
